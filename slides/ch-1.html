Section 1.1

Statistics is a discipline that guides researchers in collecting, exploring, and drawing conclusions from data. This course will demonstrate the statistical investigation process for a variety of fields of application.

The six steps of a statistical investigation

The six steps of a statistical investigation is a framework for learning from data. The six steps are a guide for collecting and interpreting data in keeping with the scientific method. When research questions are investigated with the six-step method, conclusions are supported by systematic study rather than individual experiences. The six steps are described below.

1. Ask a research question.

Confirm the question can be addressed by collecting data. Typical questions involve comparing groups, asking how two characteristics are related, or surveying people’s opinions.

2. Design a study and collect data.

Plan how to select the people or objects to be studied, decide how to gather the relevant data, and carry out the data collection in a careful, systematic manner.

3. Explore the data.

Look for patterns that relate to the research question or raise additional questions.

4. Draw inferences beyond the data.

Determine whether any findings in the data reflect a genuine tendency and estimate the strength of that tendency.

5. Formulate conclusions

Consider whether inferences made in step 4 can be generalized to processes or groups beyond those represented in the study. Identify whether a cause-and-effect conclusion is warranted.

6. Look back and ahead.

Point out limitations of the study and suggest new studies to build on the findings.

Asking a research question

State a research question you would be interested in answering.

What affects the choice to become an organ donor?

Researchers Johnson and Goldstein asked 161 volunteer participants to imagine moving to a new state and applying for a driver’s license. One part of the application asked whether the participants would agree to be an organ donor. Participants were presented with one of three different forms of the question:

  • You will not be a donor unless you opt in.
  • You will be a donor unless you opt out.
  • Choose whether or not to be a donor (neutral).

Basic Terminology

Basic Terminology

  • Data are the values measured or categories recorded on individual objects of interest.

  • The observational unit is the individual object on which data are recorded.

  • A variable is a characteristic being recorded about the observational units.

    • A quantitative variable has numeric values for which ordinary arithmetic (addition, multiplication) makes sense.

    • A categorical variable places observational units into categories.

  • A distribution is the pattern of outcomes of a variable.

The best way to determine if a variable is quantitative or categorical is to ask the question, “Can I take the average of this? And would that make sense?” For example, I could ask a group of people to tell me what ZIP code they currently live in, and I could technically take the average of those numbers, but it wouldn’t make sense to do so. That is because ZIP codes are categorical.

Section 1.2

In this section, step 3 of the Statistical Investigation Process is further explored. The goal is to describe, measure, and, if possible, explain the patterns of variation (the distribution) in the values of the variable.

Graphs for quantitative variables

A dotplot is a graph with one dot representing the variable outcome for each observational unit. Dotplots, like the graph of the performance lengths in the animation above, are good for smaller datasets. Another common graph for a quantitative variable is a histogram. A histogram separates the quantitative variable into bins and displays the number of observations in each bin.

Describing the distribution

  • The shape of a distribution is described by the number of peaks and whether one side of the distribution tends to stretch out longer than the other.
  • The center of a distribution is the middle or typical value.
  • The variability of a distribution is the spread in the observations.
  • Unusual observations are observations that do not follow the overall pattern of the distribution.

Section 1.3: Preliminary: Exploring random processes

Random processes

A random process is a repeatable process where the result for an individual attempt is not known in advance but there is a predictable pattern to the results in the long run. Playing Let’s Make a Deal is an example of a random process: The result for the next round of the game is uncertain, but if the game is played many, many times, a predictable pattern is expected.

A simulation is an artificial re-creation of a random process.

Interpretation of probability

The probability of an outcome is the long-run proportion of times an outcome from a random process occurs if the process is repeated indefinitely under identical conditions. A probability can be written as a decimal (0.3333), a fraction (1/3), or a percentage (33.33%).
