    Section 10.2 Comparing multiple means: Theory-based approach

    Subsection Comparing popular diets

    Because about two-thirds of Americans are considered overweight, weight loss is big business. There are many different types of diets, but do some work better than others? Is low fat better than low carb or is some combination best? Researchers (Garnder et al. 2007) conducted a study involving four popular diets: Atkins (very low carb), Zone (40:30:30 ratio of carbs, protein, fat), LEARN (high carbohydrate, low fat), and Ornish (low fat). They randomly assigned women aged 25-50 with a body mass index (BMI) of 27-40 (overweight and obese) to one of the four diets. The 311 women who volunteered for the program were educated on their assigned diet and were observed periodically as they stayed on the diet for a year. At the end of the year, the researchers calculated the change in BMI (e.g., negative means reduction in BMI) for each woman and compared the results across the four diets.

    🔗
    🔗
    Although the Mean Group Diff statistic is fairly easy to understand and calculate, it is not commonly used, in part because there is no theory-based model for the null distribution. Another downside is that the Mean Group Diff statistic is not standardized so it is not comparable across studies (e.g., a Mean Group Diff statistic of 1 in one study might be strong evidence but in another study might not show convincing evidence against the null hypothesis).
    🔗

    Key Idea 10.2.8.

    Instead of comparing only the differences in means, a statistic could standardize those differences by comparing to the amount of within-group or natural variability in the response variable while also taking sample sizes into account.
    🔗
    🔗
    A much more commonly used statistic for comparing multiple groups on a quantitative response, which does have a theory-based distribution, is called an F-statistic. As with the Mean Group Diff statistic, the F-statistic equals zero only when the group means are all identical. Otherwise the F-statistic is positive, with larger values indicating larger differences across the group means.
    🔗
    The F-statistic (see Formula section at the end of Example 9.2 for details) is a ratio of “between-group” and “within-group” variability. Thus,
    \begin{equation*} F = \frac{\textbf{Between-group variability}}{\textbf{Within-group variability}} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Exercise 10.2.17.

    Now we’ll use the Squared Error (SE) terms from the last exercise to compute the ANOVA table.
    🔗

    (a)

    First, we’ll fill in the df column, which stands for “degrees of freedom”. For the first entry, corresponding to the Model row, input the number of groups we have minus 1. For the last entry, corresponding to the Total row, input the sample size minus 1. For the middle entry, corresponding to the Error row, input \(\text{df}_{\text{Total}} - \text{df}_{\text{Model}}\text{,}\) in other words, the last number from the column minus the first number from this column.
    🔗
    Your three numbers should be such that the number from the Model row plus the number from the Error row add up to the number from the Total row.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Now we’ll fill in the SS (SSE) column, the Sum Squared Error. For the first number, the Model row, compute the sum of four Squared Error (SE) numbers from the last exercise. For the last number, the Total row, compute \(s^2 \times n\text{,}\) in other words, the standard deviation of the entire sample times the sample size of the whole sample. For the middle entry, corresponding to the Error row, compute \(\text{SS}_{\text{Total}}-\text{SS}_{\text{Model}}\text{,}\) in other words, the last entry of this column minus the first entry.
    🔗
    Just like part a, your three numbers should be such that the number from the Model row plus the number from the Error row add up to the number from the Total row.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Now we’ll fill in the MS (MSE) column, the mean squared error column. For the Model and the Error columns, take the SS (SSE) number to the left divided by the df number further left, i.e. \(\text{MS} = \frac{\text{SS}}{\text{df}}\text{.}\) Leave the bottom cell empty because there is no mean squared error for the Total row.
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    We’re finally read to compute \(F\text{.}\) We just need the two numbers from the MS (MSE) column. Take the first number, i.e. the Model number, and divide by the second number, i.e. the Error number.
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    The F-statistic computes the between-group-variability divided by the within-group-variability. Write something you notice (i.e. a statement) or something you wonder (i.e. a question) about how it is computed or what it represents.
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Now we’ll compute the last number, the \(R^2\) number. We just need the first and last numbers of the SS (SSE) column. \(R^2\) is simply the first number divided by the last number from the SS (SSE) column.
    🔗
    The p-value takes a more complicated computation than is necessary for this exercise. In this case, it is approximately 0.0106.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Another way to summarize the usefulness of the explanatory variable, is the “coefficient of determination,” or \(R^2\) value.
    🔗

    Definition 10.2.24.

    The proportion of the variation in the response variable which is explained (accounted for) by the treatment (groups) is called
    \begin{equation*} R^2 = 1 - \frac{ \text{within-group variability} }{ \text{total variability} } \text{.} \end{equation*}

    Subsection Follow-up analysis

    The ANOVA F-test is an overall test. A significant test result tells us that we have strong evidence that at least one population mean is different from the others but does not tell us which one(s) differ from which other one(s). To determine this we need to apply a follow-up procedure to ANOVA. There are many different follow-up tests that can be done. We will use pairwise confidence intervals for the difference in two means, as we did before.
    🔗

    Key Idea 10.2.27.

    If an ANOVA test finds evidence that there is at least one long-run or population mean that is different, there are many different follow-up tests designed to help pinpoint where difference(s) occur. One option is to look at all the pairwise theory-based confidence intervals for the difference in long-run or population means.

    Subsection Validity conditions

    As with other theory-based approaches of conducting tests of significance, certain validity conditions must be met in order to conduct an ANOVA F-test.
    🔗

    Validity Conditions 10.2.31 for the ANOVA F-test.

    The F-distribution is a good approximation to the null distribution of the F-statistic as long as:
    • Either the sample size is at least 20 for all the groups, without strong skewness or outliers; or if the sample size is less than 20, then the distribution of the response variable is approximately symmetric in all the samples (examine the dotplots for skewness or outliers).
      🔗
    • The standard deviations of the samples are approximately equal to each other (largest standard deviation is not more than twice the value of the smallest standard deviation).
      🔗
    🔗
